# B1 — The Glacier cost gotcha

What's the actual cost impact of an early deletion after archival? How would you redesign the
lifecycle so this stops quietly costing more than budgeted?

## The actual cost impact

`storage_class = "GLACIER"` is S3 Glacier **Flexible Retrieval**, which has a **90-day minimum
storage duration**. An object deleted, overwritten or moved out before 90 days is charged a
pro-rated **early-deletion fee for the remaining days**, as if it had stayed the full 90.

Timeline in the scenario:

| Day | Event | Billing |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Photo uploaded | S3 Standard |
| 30 | Lifecycle transitions it to Glacier | Lifecycle transition request fee per object; 90-day Glacier clock starts |
| 40 | Seller requests deletion and we delete immediately | 10 days actually stored in Glacier, **80 days billed as early deletion** |

Per GB, at approximate us-east-1 list prices (Standard ≈ $0.023/GB-month, Glacier Flexible ≈
$0.0036/GB-month):

- Glacier storage actually used: 10 days ≈ **$0.0012**
- Glacier storage actually billed: 90 days ≈ **$0.0108**, which is **9×** the usage
  (early-delete portion ≈ $0.0096)
- For comparison, leaving it in Standard for those 10 days ≈ $0.0077. Archiving and then deleting
  cost **~40% more than never archiving at all**, before per-object fees.

Per-object costs make this worse for photos:
- **Transition request fee** for every object moved to Glacier, whether it's later deleted or not.
  It's charged per object, not per GB, so millions of photos add up.
- **~40 KB of billable overhead per archived object** (index and metadata: about 32 KB at the
  Glacier rate plus 8 KB at the Standard rate). For thumbnails and small images this can be a
  large fraction of, or more than, the image itself.

So the budget assumption that "Glacier is ~6× cheaper than Standard" only holds for objects that
**stay at least 90 days after archival**. Sold-listing photos are exactly the population most
likely to get a deletion request soon after the sale, so a meaningful share of them never reach
that point. The result is a cost that **quietly exceeds the budget**: it shows up as an
early-delete line item (`EarlyDelete` usage types in the Cost and Usage Report) rather than as
storage, and nobody models it up front.

Two things that are *not* the fix:
- **Don't delay deletion to avoid the fee.** The deletion must happen within 30 days of the
  request (a privacy and legal obligation). It also saves nothing: keeping the object until day
  120 costs exactly the same 90 days of Glacier storage. The deletion isn't the mistake. **The
  mistake is archiving objects that have a high chance of being deleted within 90 days.**
- **Don't move the object out of Glacier before deleting it.** That adds a restore or retrieval
  charge and still triggers the same early-deletion fee.

Another bug in the original rule: it has **no `filter`**, so it archives **every** object in
`haven-listing-media` 30 days after upload. That includes photos of *active* listings, which
would then need a Glacier restore (minutes to hours) before they could be served.

## Redesign (implemented in `terraform/lifecycle.tf`)

1. **Scope the rule to sold listings only.** Filter on an object tag `listing_status = sold`,
   which the app sets via `PutObjectTagging` when a listing sells. Active listing photos stay hot.
2. **Use a class with no minimum duration during the deletion-risk window.** Day 30 →
   `INTELLIGENT_TIERING` instead of `GLACIER`. Intelligent-Tiering has **no minimum storage
   duration, no early-delete fee and no retrieval fee**. It still moves untouched objects to
   Infrequent Access after 30 days and Archive Instant Access after 90 days by itself, so most of
   the savings remain. The cost is a small per-object monitoring fee.
3. **Archive deeply only after deletions have mostly stopped.** Day 180 → `GLACIER`. By then the
   90-day minimum mostly overlaps time the photo would have been kept anyway. Tune 180 from real
   data: plot the distribution of deletion requests by days since sale, and set the Glacier
   transition past the point where most requests have arrived, plus a buffer.
4. **Skip tiny objects.** `object_size_greater_than = 128 KB`. Below that, Intelligent-Tiering
   doesn't auto-tier, and Glacier's per-object overhead and transition fees cost more than they
   save. Thumbnails stay in Standard.
5. **Make deletion actually delete.** If versioning is (or later gets) enabled, a delete only
   creates a delete marker. The archived bytes keep billing as a noncurrent version *and* the
   30-day deletion requirement isn't met. Added a rule that expires noncurrent versions after
   1 day, plus cleanup of incomplete multipart uploads.

Alternative if the access pattern is well known: `STANDARD_IA` at day 30 (30-day minimum, so a
day-40 deletion costs at most ~20 extra IA-days) before `GLACIER` later. I chose
Intelligent-Tiering because it removes the minimum-duration risk completely.

### Make it visible so it can't stay quiet

- Budget for the early-delete and transition line items explicitly, using the observed deletion
  rate × average remaining days.
- Add an AWS Budgets alert, or a CUR/Cost Explorer filter, on the `EarlyDelete` and transition
  usage types for this bucket, tagged with `landlord_account_id` so the cost is attributed to
  the billing unit (ties into B2).
- Review the real deletion-request curve every quarter and adjust the day-180 Glacier transition.
